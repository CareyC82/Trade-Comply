## Post topic and source checked

Rotated away from yesterday's Amazon TIC workflow to a practical lithium-battery shipping evidence check. PHMSA's 2024 test-summary guide says a UN 38.3 test summary has standardized traceability information, including manufacturer and lab details, unique report ID/date, battery type/model/description and watt-hour rating, tests/results, and responsible person. The post below is explicitly about transport-design traceability; it does not equate a UN 38.3 summary with retail-product certification or marketplace approval.

Primary sources:

- PHMSA, Lithium Battery Test Summaries: https://www.phmsa.dot.gov/sites/phmsa.dot.gov/files/2024-09/Lithium-Battery-Test-Summary-2024.pdf
- PHMSA, Transporting Lithium Batteries: https://www.phmsa.dot.gov/lithiumbatteries

## Draft for LinkedIn — publish only after checking today's feed

> For a lithium-battery shipment, “the supplier sent a UN 38.3 document” is a starting point—not the end of the evidence check.
>
> Before relying on a test summary, compare it with the battery actually installed or shipped:
>
> • battery model and chemistry;
> • watt-hour rating or lithium content;
> • manufacturer and test-lab details;
> • unique report ID and test date; and
> • tests/results and the responsible person listed.
>
> PHMSA’s test-summary guide explains that these details make the battery design traceable for transport. That is a transport-safety evidence check; it does not by itself establish that the finished product meets every destination-market or marketplace requirement.
>
> TraceWize is an early-stage pre-screen to help organize product facts and evidence questions. It is not a certification, legal opinion, shipping authorization, or marketplace approval.
>
> When you receive a battery test summary, what is hardest to match to the product in hand: model, watt-hour rating, or manufacturer identity?
>
> PHMSA guide: https://www.phmsa.dot.gov/sites/phmsa.dot.gov/files/2024-09/Lithium-Battery-Test-Summary-2024.pdf
