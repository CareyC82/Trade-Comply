## Before Compliance Review Begins: Five Product Facts That Should Travel With the SKU

When a product is referred for a trade or product-compliance review, the first obstacle is often not a difficult rule. It is uncertainty about what, exactly, is being reviewed.

A supplier may have a test report, a buyer may have a marketplace listing, and an importer may have a shipment description. Each can be accurate on its own while still referring to different product versions. A model suffix may distinguish a battery configuration. A color or plug option may change only the packaging—or may indicate a different electrical component. A report can cover a product family but not make that scope obvious to the person who needs to act on it.

The practical goal is not to make sourcing or operations teams decide regulatory questions they are not qualified to decide. It is to make the handoff to the qualified reviewer specific enough that the reviewer can decide what applies, identify what is missing, and explain the next step without first reconstructing the product from several disconnected files.

### 1. Preserve the product identity

Start with the exact commercial SKU and the manufacturer's model identifier. Record the names as they appear in the purchase order, product label, supplier specification, and any report. If those identifiers differ, preserve the difference rather than silently normalizing them.

Add the supplier or factory identifier and the date the product information was confirmed. A short note such as “seller SKU X maps to manufacturer model Y, confirmed by supplier on [date]” is more useful than a folder containing files with no explanation of which item they cover.

### 2. Capture the configuration that could change the review

Record only the product attributes relevant to the review at hand. Depending on the product, that may include power source, battery chemistry and whether the battery is installed or packed separately, radio functions, included accessories, adapter or plug variant, materials, intended user, or packaging configuration.

Do not assume that a family name proves two versions are identical. Conversely, do not assume that every cosmetic variant needs a separate test. The reviewer should determine whether a difference matters. The intake record should make the difference visible.

### 3. Tie each evidence file to a claim and a version

For every supplier document, note its title, issuer, document or report number, issue date, revision, covered model(s), and source. If a field is not present, mark it “not shown” rather than filling it by inference.

Where a document is being used to support a particular product attribute, state the connection explicitly: for example, “this report is being offered as evidence for model Y's radio function.” That statement does not establish that the report is sufficient or acceptable. It simply gives the reviewer a clear proposition to assess.

Keep the original file and record when it was received. A filename such as `test-final-new.pdf` says little about provenance or revision. A consistent index is easier to update and easier to challenge when a new version arrives.

### 4. Name the destination and the business context

“For export” or “for the U.S.” may not be enough context. Record the destination country or market, intended sales channel, and whether the inquiry concerns product-market requirements, a marketplace request, shipment documentation, or another issue. Those questions may involve different decision-makers and different evidence.

If the product will be sold through more than one route or channel, list them separately. Do not infer that an answer for one market or platform automatically answers another. A pre-review intake should expose the scope of the question, not imply universal clearance.

### 5. Separate known facts, open questions, and accountable owners

Use three simple statuses:

- **Known:** supported by a cited source or identified document.
- **Needs confirmation:** a supplier, importer, broker, platform, or specialist must confirm it.
- **Not applicable / not known:** explicitly out of scope or unavailable, with the reason recorded where useful.

For each unresolved item, name the next owner and the question they need to answer. “Need compliance docs” is difficult to action. “Supplier: confirm whether model Y and model Y-Pro share the same battery pack; attach the current specification” is concrete. “Broker: confirm which entry data field they need for the applicable certificate filing” identifies a different owner and a different task.

This distinction matters in delegated workflows. For example, CPSC's importer guidance explains that an importer of record for eFiling may be an owner, purchaser, or authorized customs broker; when a broker lacks sufficient product knowledge, it may identify the owner, purchaser, or consignee responsible for certificate requirements. That is a reminder to coordinate accurate product knowledge and responsibilities—not a reason to assume the broker, supplier, or seller can answer every question for the others. See [CPSC eFiling Resources for Importers](https://www.cpsc.gov/efiling/importers).

### A small handoff table can prevent a large reconstruction exercise

| Field | Example of a useful entry |
|---|---|
| Seller SKU / manufacturer model | Seller SKU A17 → model MX-4; supplier confirmed 2026-11-20 |
| Configuration | USB-C; rechargeable battery installed; radio function present |
| Market and channel | United States; online marketplace; commercial import |
| Evidence offered | Report R-104, revision 2, issued 2026-04-12; lists model MX-4 |
| Open question | Supplier to confirm whether MX-4B uses the same battery pack |
| Next owner | Supplier for model comparison; specialist to assess evidence scope |

The values above are illustrative only. The table is not a certification checklist and does not establish that the cited evidence meets any legal, customs, safety, or marketplace requirement.

### The boundary is as important as the checklist

A well-organized intake is not an approval. It cannot establish that a product complies, that a report is accepted by a regulator or marketplace, that a customs entry is correct, or that a shipment may proceed. Those decisions belong to the responsible party, regulator, platform, customs broker, testing laboratory, or qualified adviser as applicable.

The value of the intake is narrower and more practical: it helps the right person review the right product version against the right question, with the source and ownership of unresolved facts visible. That can reduce avoidable back-and-forth while preserving the specialist's role in making the actual determination.
